
# Unusual heroes of the Church

This is a list of people who had ministries that were remarkably odd.

- This is a partial list. You can consult the full list later when the Great Recordkeeper releases it.

## Justin Martyr

At some point around 130 AD, Justin had been seeking philosophical answers but couldn't find anything sufficient among the Greek and Roman thought of the time. He met an old Christian who was possibly a Syrian who gave him testimony about the prophets as being more reliable than the philosophers in discovering truth.

The old man's argument drove him to renounce his former religion and philosophical background to follow the Divine. He became convinced of the moral and spiritual superiority of the Christian doctrine, and decided to travel the land spreading the knowledge of Christianity as the true philosophy.

He traveled all over teaching as a customary philosopher, and started his own school. He wrote formal philosophical defenses of Christianity called [Apologies](spiritual-exercises-evangelism-apologetics.md) that indicated it was the fulfillment of [Greek philosophy](philosophy.md).

He was tried and beheaded by Junius Rusticus under Marcus Aurelius' reign sometime between 162-168. To this day, the word "martyr" is a broad term for what he had done.

## Telemachus

Somewhere around the year 391 or 404, Telemachus was an Eastern ascetic who went to Rome. He went to a Roman amphitheatre and tried to stop a gladiatorial fight to the death.

The details are hazy, but he obviously created a stir, and the crowd stoned him to death. The Christian Emperor Honorius, however, was impressed enough by Telemachus' martyrdom that he set a historic ban on gladiator fights.

## Vitalis of Gaza

Vitalis was a monk born in Gaza and served in the monastery of Seridus. At the age of 60, he traveled to Alexandria.

His legend states that he obtained the name and address of every prostitute in the city, and then hired himself out as a day laborer. At the end of each day, he would take his wage to one of those women for the night and, according to the legend, would spend the night awake and praying.

Apparently, as he approached the homes of the prostitutes, he would loudly proclaim where he was intending to visit. When he was asked why he was visiting prostitutes, he would say, "Have I not a body like everyone else? Are monks not like other men? Mind your own business!"

A man who apparently misunderstood the nature of his brothel visits struck him on the head. Vitalis was able to return to his hut before he died. Apparently during his burial, former prostitutes explained his ministry. He was venerated in the Catholic Church as the patron saint of prostitutes and day laborers.

## Winfrid/Boniface

In 716, the English missionary Winfred began going to pagan Frisia and Germania (modern-day Germany). He was renamed Boniface by the pope and appointed missionary bishop for Germania, which didn't have any church organization whatsoever.

The Donar Oak was a pagan sacred tree that believers sacrificed a baby to every year, and they believed anyone who chopped it down would be struck dead by Thor. Boniface began to chop it down, and an early biographer said a great wind suddenly blew the tree over. The people were amazed and converted to Christianity, and he built a church from its wood at the site that became the Fritzlar monastery.

Boniface wasn't necessarily alone (there was a nearby fortified settlement nearby), but he was able to create many converts by proving their god had no power.

## Ramon Llull

In 1263, Ramon was a married man, and had been writing a romantic poem for another married woman when he saw a vision of Jesus Christ on the cross. The vision came 5 times over several days, and he conceded the final time.

He went to confession and began to permanently change his life. He modeled Francis of Assisi, sold everything he had, and went on pilgrimages to various places. When he returned he purchased a Muslim slave to learn Arabic from him, then spent 9 years studying Latin and Arabic theology and philosophy in relative solitude.

He founded the first missionary training school that focused on learning the target group's native language. He also was a prolific writer and ended up writing 265 books in Catalan, Latin, and Arabic.

He traveled to North Africa 3 times, and was stoned by a crowd in Tunisia in 1316. He died on the ship taking him home at age 83.

## Charles Foucald

In 1883, Charles left a life of partying as French nobility to quietly study Algeria and Morocco. He saw Muslims praying and asked God in a private prayer for him to come to know Him.

In 1890, Charles joined a Trappist monastery. He stayed in that life until 1901, but had an issue with how the residents of the surrounding villages had a worse life than the monks.

Once he was newly ordained in 1901 as a Catholic priest, he traveled to live near the Tuareg people in southern Algeria. It was a brutal experience acclimating to the harsh environment, but he spent over a decade there learning their language and culture.

He sat with sick children, shared his food during droughts, and listened to disputes without taking sides or passing judgment. The Tuareg eventually called him marabout, or "holy man". He also compiled the first comprehensive Tuareg-French dictionary still used to this day.

In 1916, bandits raided his home and killed him at age 58. He made zero converts.

However, his journals started circulating, and religious communities started following his example. He is now an inspiration to many within the Catholic church.

## Edward J. Flanagan

In 1916, Edward was an Irish-born Catholic Priest in the Diocese of Omaha, Nebraska. He started by serving at a workingman's hotel in the downtown district. He offered food and a dry bed to many men working the railroads and stockyards, and noticed many of their stories' ruin began when they were orphaned, arrested for vagrancy as children, and then locked in the county jail.

He started visiting the courthouse and was moved heavily by the legal system's treatment of young children, since they would prosecute children as if they were adults (meaning they'd spend time incarcerated with the same sentencing). The system itself would indirectly create hardened criminals, since the children would find [father figures](parenting-children.md) while they served time.

In 1917, he decided to open a home for homeless boys. While his Archbishop had misigivings, he received an endorsement from the Catholic Church, and started by interrupting a sentencing hearing to claim custody for five boys.

The local authorities tolerated the experiment for three months, and then the judges started sending dozens of boys. He had to face issues like city inspectors and fire department citations for overcrowding, as well as the landlord doubling the rent. All of this was funded by Edward's dubious financial management, such as writing bad checks, begging for coal, and borrowing money from pawnbrokers. He was frequently thousands of dollars in debt to local grocers and supply companies, and wrote hundreds of letters to wealthy donors.

Edward didn't believe in the reform school model, which he thought was too punitive, and stated, "there's no such thing as a bad boy", and would eventually fire any staff member who used "bad" to describe a child. Three boys ran away one time, with one of them stealing money directly from Edward's desk. He was pressured to sign the paperwork to release control to the government, but he tore the paperwork in half.

He moved the entire operation out of the city limits to an active dairy farm, which isolated the boys from the city government's jurisdiction. This became Boys Town: a large community of mostly boys aged 10-16 with its own boy-mayor, schools, chapel, post office, cottages, gymnasium, and other facilities. He gave the heaviest responsibilities to the boys with the worst criminal records, and the children managed the dairy cattle and harvested corn.

He established a legal trust and demanded permanent legal custody of the children. The courts continued sending their worst cases to him, but Boys Town persisted.

His work became widely known, including a movie made about his life in 1938, and he was allowed to review the script before filming. Boys Town had about 500 ciitzens by that time.

Edward died of a heart attack in 1948 while trying to establish youth programs in Germany for children displaced by World War II.

His impact went farther. The state of Nebraska stopped sending orphaned children to the adult penitentiary and studied the farm's structure as a blueprint for the modern juvenile justice system. While Boys Town has been steadily involved in some sort of financial scandal about every few decades, the organization is still active to this day.

## Gino Bartali

Gino was a champion road cyclist, and was one of the most famous cyclists before World War II. He was raised in a religious family, and his belief gave him the nickname "Gino the Pious". He prayed before meals and resented when his teammates swore.

Since he was famous, he used his fame to carry messages for the Italian Resistance and the Assisi network, as well as forged identification documents. He would do this by hiding the messages in the frame and handlebars of his bicycle.  He would travel all over Italy with his racing jersey, and said he was training for an upcoming race. He wouldn't permit the police to search his bike because it was "perfectly calibrated for the race", and they were afraid to arrest him out of fear of public backlash.

He ended being arrested by Nazi intelligence and taken for questioning, but he didn't reveal what he had done. He kept working even after the threats to his life, and led Jewish refugees to the Swiss Alps himself. He pulled a wagon with a secret compartment and told patrols it was simply part of his training.

He told his son Andrea, "One does these things and then that's that." Even after the war, he didn't talk about what he did, and used to say, "Some medals are made to hang on the soul, not the jacket."

During the war, he had also apparently hidden a Jewish family in his cellar and thereby saved their lives.

He stopped racing when he was 40 after a road accident injury, and had lost much of his money by then. He died of a heart attack in 2000. Since he kept lots of it private, there is quite a bit of debate over how much he *actually* did, though it's very clear he saved many lives.

## Brother Andrew

Andrew "Anne" van der Bijl visited Communist Poland in 1955 and learned that the Soviet Union had very few Bibles. He went on a government-controlled tour to Czechoslovakia (the only way to visit the country) and left the tour to meet with local Christian Groups. Later that year he founded Open Doors to make his mission.

In 1957, he traveled to Moscow with a Volkswagen Beetle, which was gifted by an older couple, the Whetstras, and he had to learn to drive.

When he smuggled Bibles, which were severely illegal contraband, he would often place the material in view when he had to stop at police checkpoints, which was a gesture of his trust that God would protect him. By the time his Volkswagen was finally decommissioned, he estimated it had gone over 1 million miles.

He also visited China in the 1960s after Mao's Cultural Revolution created the Bamboo Curtain, as well as Cuba after the Cuban Revolution (since the country didn't require visas from Dutch citizens).

At the time, several Christian organizations like the American Bible Society and Southern Baptist Convention's Foreign Mission Board considered his Bible smuggling dangerous and ineffective because the Bibles were "freely on sale" in many Iron Curtain (Soviet) countries. Ultimately, KGB informers infiltrated his organization and tracked him.

He released his book, God's Smuggler, in 1967, and the press attention meant he had to shift to evangelism and fundraising campaigns for Open Doors. He then focused on the Middle East later in his life, including visiting Hamas and PLO leaders, where he was able to open a Christian bookstore in the [Gaza Strip](history-israel.md), as well as speak at the Islamic University of Gaza. He also attempted to meet with the Taliban, and denounced American evangelical Christians who supported the Afghanistan/Iraq wars.

He died at age 94 in 2022, and Open Doors is still active.

## José Maria Prada

In 1991, José had been a simple missionary priest for over 20 years, and was serving in Brazil at the Saint Anthony Parish.

A man asked him and asked to be married in the Church. Per procedure, he investigated the situation and saw the man was already sacramentally married to another woman. He refused to marry him.

The man persisted and returned several times. He tried pressuring, offering money, and eventually threatening him as a military police sergeant. However, José wouldn't yield. According to reports, he said he'd rather die than celebrate the marriage.

On April 29 that year, the man returned and shot him dead at age 63. At his funeral, they carried the shirt he wore when he was killed. His story has been associated with John the Baptist, who was also killed for refusing to legitimize an unlawful union.

## Sam Childers

In 1992, Sam became a Christian. He had been a motorcycle gang member beforehand with tons of street experience, and went to a Sudanese mission trip in 1998 where he observed the dictator Joseph Kony recruiting children for his army. In his righteous anger, he sought to kill Kony, and founded Angels of East Africa, which was housing an orphanage in the middle of the Southern Sudanese warzone.

There is much more to the story in his book Another Man's War. Suffice to say, he saw military action at least a few times.

## Shahbaz Bhatti

Clement Shahbaz Bhatti was a Pakistani politician born in 1968, and the first Christian Pakistani Federal Minister for Minorities Affairs.

Starting in 2009, he had received death threats by supporting the Pakistani Christians attacked in Gojra that year. The threats increased when he supported Asia Bibi, who was a Christian sentenced to death for blasphemy.

He predicted his death and recorded a video in advance, stating "I believe in Jesus Christ who has given his own life for us, and I am ready to die for a cause. I'm living for my community...and I will die to defend their rights."

His vehicle was sprayed with bullets when he was traveling to work through a residential district. He was pronounced dead as soon as he arrived at the hospital. His brother Paul became the new Minister of National Harmony and Minority Affairs.

In response to his death, protesters reacted peacefully by burning tires and demanding justice. To this day, he is still remembered as a martyr.
