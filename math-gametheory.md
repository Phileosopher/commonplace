
# Game theory

All [human purposes](purpose.md), when measurable, are a game, complete with implied [constraints](people-rules.md), resources, and [goals](mind-decisions.md). Most of these purposes involve other players, each with separate purposes and resources.

Game theory is an excellent subdivision of math that explores [situational ethics](morality.md) and [decision-making](mind-decisions.md). It has a [philosophical](philosophy.md) use, but will *never* be a reliable and practical predictor of human behavior and [trends](people-trends.md) relative to basic human common sense, for several reasons:

1. All mathematical elements must be theoretically measurable. [Feelings](mind-feelings.md), though, are impossible to measure because they're relative to each person's scope and interpretation of experience.
2. Even if game theory predictions could completely quantify [stories](stories-why.md) from humanity's feelings, most people would change their motivations as soon as they knew both that their past had been quantified and that they were being predicted from it.
3. Players wish to win games, but real-life people are more concerned about resources, relationships, and others' resources compared to themselves, so the win conditions move around second-by-second. Resources can move around and are usually easily measurable, but anything about human relationships is based on mutual [feelings](mind-feelings.md), thereby compounding non-measurable Problem #1.
4. Real-life people often find meta-purposes to [creatively](mind-creativity.md) hack games. Some of them are [taboo](morality-taboo.md) (e.g., con artistry) and others are [venerated](people-image-why.md) (e.g., inventing). Meta-games complicate things further than they already are, and it's unlikely any human can [visualize](mind-imagination.md) all the elements, even with [technological assistance](data-viz.md), since they're still *part* of that meta-game.
5. Purposes move around as people [change](people-changes.md), and the entire collection of a person's memories will determine subtle differentiations proportional to their [wisdom](mind-understanding.md). In any game longer than a few minutes, any modeled [expectations](mind-imagination.md) of what someone will do leans heavily into a mixture of [culture](people-culture.md) and [decisions](mind-decisions.md), which is impossible to measure and weight correctly.
6. If anyone behaves sacrificially (i.e., out of [love](people-love.md)), the game is impossible to calculate in any useful way because it's difficult to gauge *whose* interests drive actions.

Therefore, game theory is a great measurement when people are blindly acting on self-interested habit in a public forum, but that's it.

- Most game theory is built as if the games were like chess, with full information available and the only constraint being one's mental ability to use [logic](logic.md).
- In real life, games are more like poker, where there is [deception](people-lying.md), constantly missing information, and quite a bit of [luck](unknown.md).

Further, the meta-game is also incorporating a long-term strategy across multiple games.

- You could lie and coerce your way through one game, but it sabotages your ability to play many rounds of a game.
- Therefore, everyone lies and cheats, but only a little, just enough to not make everyone else collectively hate them.
- This long-term goal is the reason people play [fairly](morality-justice.md).

## Risk tolerance

Risk is the chance of having an unfavorable circumstance to oneself.

Most players will seek more risk when avoiding losses, but seek less risk when exploring gains.

- There is counterintuitive wisdom to reversing this, and seeking more risk in the course of finding gains.

## Specific games

Most of the game theory games are simply quantified [thought experiments](philosophy-paradoxes.md).

### Hobson's Choice

This is a simple game of providing two choices for the player:

1. Take something favorable that has been predetermined from a larger group, and may not be the most favorable for the player.
2. Take nothing at all.

In some ways, it's a "no duh" game, since the second choice is obviously least favorable.

But, in another, it definitely alludes to the implicit reality of [the social contract](people-contracts.md). That is, that nothing is technically ever free.
