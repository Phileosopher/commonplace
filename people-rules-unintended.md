
# Historically proven unintended consequences

Format:

1. The problem
2. The solution
3. The solution's overlooked reality
4. The consequences
5. If applicable, other consequences

NOTE: unless otherwise stated, most of these are the US government.

Siege tax (1585)

1. The Spanish army was sieging Antwerp, which involved them shelling merchant ships and causing food prices to rise.
2. Antwerp set strict price controls on food.
3. Companies will do what they can to maximize profit with the least amount of effort possible.
4. Merchant ships went to absolutely anywhere else that wasn't Antwerp to get the same price without the risks of getting shelled. People also hoarded food supplies due to the low price controls.
5. Antwerp had to surrender shortly afterward.

Hearth tax (1662)

1. Spain needed more tax revenue.
2. Charles II charged a hearth tax to generate more revenue based on the number of fireplaces, which does scale with wealth.
3. People don't like to pay taxes and will adapt their lifestyle to avoid it.
4. Many people stopped up their chimneys to avoid the tax. One woman broke through to her neighbor's chimney to use it, which caused a large fire that killed 20 people. The tax revenue was also less than expected.

Window tax (1696)

1. England needed more tax revenue.
2. King William III taxed windows to generate more revenue, which does scale with wealth and is easy to observe.
3. People don't like to pay taxes and will adapt their lifestyle to avoid it.
4. Houses were then built with fewer windows and existing windows were bricked up. In tenement housing, this led to a lack of ventilation and increased disease and stunted growth in children, which is a portion of the issues indicated by Charles Dickens.

Maritime taxes (1773)

1. The British government needs more money.
2. Britain collected port and lighthouse fees based on their length and width.
3. People don't like to pay taxes and will adapt their lifestyle to avoid it.
4. Citizens responded by building deep, sluggish, flat-bottomed, flat-sided vessels. This made them unstable to navigate.

No railroad (1862)

1. There was no railroad for transcontinental elites to travel.
2. The government paid money for each mile of track laid for a transcontinental railroad.
3. Companies will do what they can to maximize profit.
4. Since nobody was paying attention, Union Pacific arbitrarily lengthened how much track they laid by making it a bow shape. After two and a half years of construction, the track only covered 45 miles' distance.

Rat problem (1883)

1. Rats in Hawaii were damaging sugar crops.
2. The government introduced a natural predator to rats with the Small Indian Mongoose.
3. Nature is an interconnected web with a food chain and many interconnected feedback loops.
4. Rats are primarily nocturnal and mongoose are primarily active during the day, so the mongoose predated on the natural wildlife.
5. 100 years later, they were still a problem on the islands.

Prohibition (1896)

1. Alcohol was making people do bad things.
2. New York banned alcohol with the Raines law, except for hotels where the wealthy could still order their drinks paired with their meal.
3. Citizens will try to maintain their lifestyle if they can.
4. Businesses added barely functioning "Raines law hotels" to keep selling alcohol and business owners made agreements with neighboring hotels. The number of hotels in Brooklyn went from 13 to 800 within 6 months, which were especially convenient for unmarried couples and prostitutes. To comply with the meal requirement bartenders created a "Raines sandwich", which was a likely-not-edible sandwich that "came with the meal", but was then taken away frequently within seconds to be paired with the next order. It wasn't uncommon for the same sandwich to be reused for weeks.

Not enough married men (1900)

1. Argentinean bachelors were not contributing to population growth.
2. Argentina's government gave a bachelor [tax](money-accounting-taxes.md) on bachelors who stayed single, but an exemption for the ones who proposed but were rejected.
3. People don't like to pay taxes and will adapt their lifestyle to avoid it.
4. A cottage industry of female professional rejectors arose who would attest and swear to authorities that a particular man tried to propose and she rejected them. This was a fraction of the tax cost itself.

Banned cartoons (1903)

1. The politician Samuel Pennypacker was depicted as a parrot in many political cartoons.
2. The government banned depictions of any person as any type of animal.
3. [Artists](art.md) like to rebel against the system, and will find new and [creative](mind-creativity-how.md) ways to keep doing it.
4. Cartoonists depicted Samuel Pennypacker as every variety of plant and inanimate object. The bill was pulled and replaced with the Salus-Grady libel law that made printing companies personally responsible for the artists' depictions. The printers dared them to take them to court, but the law was repealed after he left office.

Paleontology finds (1937)

1. Finding human fossils is difficult.
2. Gustav Heinrich Ralph von Koenigswald paid the locals for every fragment of human fossil they could find.
3. People will do things that make them more money if it doesn't give any adverse consequences to them.
4. People were intentionally breaking apart the fossils into tiny pieces to maximize their profit. When he stopped his incentive, the people burned the remaining skulls they had as revenge.

Communist problem (1940)

1. Communists were gaining political influence.
2. Georgia banned parties from showing up on their ballots by adding requirements such as 5% of the people being part of that party, notarized and collected signatures for validation, and other bureaucratic walls.
3. People avoid doing things that won't create reasonable results.
4. It has now been quite a long time since any other third political party has run in Georgia.

Beef prices (1946)

1. The price of beef was rising quickly, and it was an election year.
2. The government reissued wartime price controls.
3. Companies have to stay profitable or they'll go out of business.
4. Meat producers didn't see a reason to sell meat, so they didn't. Everyone got angry including miners striking, butchers closing, and hospitals having to serve horse meat. President Truman repealed the law, but Democrats were destroyed in the polls and lost for the first time in 16 years.

Bird problem (1958)

1. Birds in China were eating too much grain.
2. The eurasian sparrow became labeled an enemy of the state and triggered a campaign to end the species.
3. Nature is an interconnected web with a food chain and many negative feedback loops.
4. Sparrows eat insects, who eat more grain than sparrows, and there was an insect epidemic. This caused massive famine, starvation, and deaths of about 15-45 million people.

Banned billboards (1968)

1. Billboards were cluttering the scenery in Vermont.
2. Vermont's government banned using billboards.
3. Companies really want to advertise their products, even without billboards.
4. Instead of billboards, companies put up really stupid concrete displays as "public art" such as a 12-foot gorilla holding a car, a 19-foot genie holding a carpet and a squirrel in bathing suit. The worst of this was when one resident put a 700-pound middle finger on a 16-foot pole.

Osborne reef (1972)

1. There were too many old tires in old landfills.
2. They dumped the tires into the ocean to create an artificial reef.
3. Tires are round and roll, as well as being made from highly synthetic chemicals.
4. The tires caused tremendous ecological damage, as well as releasing toxic chemicals into the water. It has persisted for decades, has required millions of dollars of cleanup, and has needed help from the military.

Fuel economy penalties (1975)

1. Cars were using too much fuel.
2. The government made penalties against auto manufacturers who failed to meet efficiency standards, with more stringent standards for cars than for trucks.
3. People will avoid having to pay fines if at all possible.
4. Car manufacturers started making their cars bigger (crossovers, SUVs, minivans). This made them classify as "trucks" and therefore avoid those penalties, and over 80% of new vehicles are now classified as trucks in the USA.

Biofuel subsidies (1980s)

1. Oil was imported in the USA and its price was going up.
2. The government funded biofuel subsidies (largely for corn) and mandate using it.
3. Companies will do what they can to maximize profit.
4. Growing lots of corn incentivizes deforestation, raises food and gas prices, causes pollution from fertilizer and pesticides, and refining it releases more toxic chemicals into the atmosphere than gasoline. Making 1 gallon of ethanol also requires 1.29 gallons of fossil fuels.

Toxin labeling (1986)

1. Toxic chemicals can harm people.
2. California introduced Prop 65, a ballot initiative that required businesses to give reasonable warning to consumers about products that may cause cancer or reproductive harm. This came with a $2,500 per day fine.
3. People don't want to pay heavy fines if it's convenient to not do so.
4. The list had 235 chemicals at first, but became over 900. Every type of company placed warning labels irrespective of the severity of risk or degree of exposure. This included bars, schools, golf clubs, toasters, lamps, children's toys, potato chips, pancakes, and trees. This meant Californians learned to ignore them.

Natural gas education (1987)

1. Natural gas is dangerous and people often aren't aware of its presence.
2. Baltimore Gas and Electric decided to educate consumers about natural gas by adding scratch and sniff stickers to energy bills.
3. Quality control is important, and is [why there are standards](standards.md).
4. The scratch and sniff stickers weren't sealed, so hundreds of residents called the fire department before even opening their mail. The company apologized and stopped the program.

Pollution problem (1989)

1. There is a pollution problem in Mexico City.
2. Mexico's government bans 1 out of 5 cars from driving each day based on the number on their license plate to cut down on emissions.
3. Citizens will try to maintain their lifestyle if they can.
4. Citizens got around the ban by buying a second car that was cheaper, older, and less efficient. This caused more emissions than before.
5. Politicians decided in 2008 to expand the program to Saturdays.

Yacht tax (1990)

1. The national debt has grown extremely high.
2. Set a 10% luxury tax on expensive boats.
3. Buyers don't like to pay taxes and will look elsewhere if they can.
4. Since middle income people made the boats, the wealthy people bought 70% of their yachts from other countries instead, drove hundreds of thousands of middle class workers out of business, and created a net loss in tax revenue.

Loonie toss (1992)

1. Canada's new dancing trends were creating immoral behavior.
2. Canada set a 1-meter buffer zone between patrons and dancers.
3. Citizens will try to maintain their lifestyle if they can.
4. Since people would pay for dances and bills didn't fly far, women had men throwing loonie coins directly at them (the "loonie toss").

Soccer ties (1994)

1. Ties in [soccer](fun-sports-large.md) are boring because nobody scores.
2. The Caribbean Cup made game-winning goals in overtime worth double.
3. People will take advantage of whatever [mathematical](math.md) advantage they can to win.
4. The Barbados-Grenada qualifying round had a surreal experience. Barbados had to win the match by at least 2 more goals than Grenada, and was up 2-1 in the last few minutes. To get the 2-point lead, they scored a goal against themselves, and the context meant that Grenada was trying to score any goal whatsoever in the last minutes while Barbados was trying to prevent either score from happening. Barbados scored in overtime, and the rule was dropped.

The Scunthorpe problem (1996)

1. People on the internet were swearing a lot.
2. AOL's administrators inserted content filters to block bad language.
3. [Language](language.md) is complex, with varying meanings for similar words.
4. Some perfectly benign words were also banned, such as the city of Scunthorpe. AOL responded by forcing the city to be renamed Sconthorpe. This has been an issue for all sorts of things like dog breeds, Pokèmon cards, and Super Bowl 30. The Scunthorpe problem is now the general term for this issue.

Not enough biofuel (2000)

1. There weren't enough people consuming alternative biofuels instead of oil.
2. Arizona gave substantial tax credits for biofuel vehicles.
3. People will pay less money for things, so companies will find ways to sell things for less.
4. New SUVs were being sold for tens of thousands of dollars less because they added a 1-gallon biofuel tank that nobody would ever likely fill up. It cost the state hundreds of millions and increased gas consumption.

Illegal poppy growth (2002)

1. Afghanistan had many people growing poppy for illegal opium synthesis.
2. Afghanistan's government made a $30 million incentive to give poppy growers $700/acre to destroy their crops.
3. People will do things that make them more money if it doesn't give any adverse consequences to them.
4. Poppy cultivation exploded in popularity. Further, many Afghans would harvest the sap from the plants before destroying the poppy, effectively being paid twice for the same crop.

Barbara Streisand (2003)

1. Barbara Streisand's house was on the internet.
2. Barbara Streisand sued the photographer for $50 million and demanded the photo be removed.
3. People are interested when someone tries to hide something.
4. Before Streisand did anything, there were only 6 views for the photo, which was for measuring coastal erosion. After her effort, over 400,000 people viewed the photo, she had to $177,000 in legal fees to her defendant, and has had [an embarrassing effect named after her](lawsaxioms.md).

Too much traffic (2003)

1. Jakarta has some of the worst traffic in the world.
2. The government introduced the 3 in 1 rule that required a driver to carry 3 people to drive on main roads.
3. Citizens will try to maintain their lifestyle if they can.
4. A cottage industry of professional jockeys rose up, with people being paid to exist in cars. Even babies were contracted for the role. The law was thrown out in 2016.

Greenhouse gases (2005)

1. Greenhouse gases might be destroying the planet.
2. The UN intergovernmental panel on climate change created a system that rewarded polluting companies with carbon credits for disposing of toxic chemicals, which could later be redeemed for cash. More harmful pollutants could be redeemed for more cash.
3. Companies will do what they can to maximize profit.
4. Hydrofluorocarbon-23 was a byproduct of the very common coolant hydrochlorofluorocarbon-22. Manufacturers in India and China ramped up production of HCFC-22 to dispose of HFC-23, which gave them tens of millions of dollars a year. Many manufacturers made twice the income from the disposal than actually selling HCFC-22. This caused [no scarcity](money-economics.md) of it and made the price for it stay very low, which motivated air conditioner and refrigerator manufacturers to continue using it instead of switching. This was so profitable that when the UN wanted to kill the program, China threatened to vent all their gas into the atmosphere if they didn't keep it going.

Not enough biofuel (2005)

1. People were consuming too much oil-derived fuel and not enough biofuels.
2. The government created alternative fuel tax credits for companies that blended oil with biofuel.
3. Companies will do what they can to maximize profit.
4. Paper companies already used a pure biofuel called black liquor, which is extracted from wood during the papermaking process. They bought oil they didn't need to mix it with their black liquor to qualify for the biofuel subsidy.

Coin by mail (2008)

1. People weren't switching to $1 coins.
2. The US Mint gave an offer where they would mail $1 coins for $1 and they'd cover shipping.
3. Companies give small incentives for customer behavior, and people like to get free things.
4. People would use credit cards to get thousands of $1 coins, then would take them immediately to the bank to redeem them. The US Mint killed the condition, but many people acquired many frequent flyer miles this way and one person allegedly purchased $800,000 coins and earned platinum elite status with American Airlines.

Gun buyback (2008)

1. Oakland, CA has too many guns.
2. Oakland's government gave $250 for every gun turned into the police, no questions asked.
3. People will do things that make them more money if it doesn't give any adverse consequences to them.
4. People turned in their defective, broken guns, and the first two people in line for the program were gun dealers offloading their worthless stock. Some people drove from out of state to turn them in. The program was estimated to cost $50,000, but they issued IOUs and the cost totaled $170,000. Also, some gun buyers were walking through the queue and offering $300 for specialty items.

Auto redemption (2009)

1. The economy was going through a major recession, and everyone should save the environment, and everyone should rely on domestic manufacturing, and an election campaign was coming up.
2. The government gave $1 billion to turn in their vehicles for new, more ecological ones.
3. People will do things that make them more money if it doesn't give any adverse consequences to them.
4. The program was very popular and burned through the money in less than a month. Congress approved $2 billion more, which was gone a month later. Further, while fuel economy improved by 0.65/gallon, US fuel consumption rose by over 800 million gallons. It also didn't last after the program ended. For every job created, the government spent $1.4 million on the program. Further, only 2 of the top 10 cars sold in the program were sold as domestic brands. It also caused the remaining used car prices to rise.

More comprehensive health insurance (2010)

1. Pre-existing conditions were excluding specific people from being able to afford health insurance.
2. The government made it illegal to use pre-existing conditions (including age) into health [insurance calculations](money-insurance-how.md).
3. Companies have to stay profitable or they'll go out of business.
4. Health insurance companies had to add that minority's costs into everyone's insurance, making it more expensive and more healthy people canceling their insurance.
5. Over time, the health insurance companies either went out of business or stopped issuing certain types of policies that didn't consider pre-existing conditions.

Traffic light electricity (~2010)

1. Traffic lights in Japan were using too much electricity.
2. Japan's government replaced their incandescent bulbs with energy-efficient LEDs that last longer.
3. LED bulbs don't radiate heat that melts ice.
4. Snow would pile up on the lights and obscure the signal. Traffic workers had to routinely use brooms to scrape it off. Others had the same issue: Greenbay, WI used bucket lifts. Saint Paul, MI used air compressors, Newark, NJ used super soakers filled with antifreeze.
5. This is still a problem in many places.

Too many accidents (2012)

1. The fatality rate on Texas roads was too high.
2. Texas' government posted an ongoing fatality statistic on highway message boards.
3. People can usually only pay attention to one thing at a time.
4. To read the sign, motorists had to pay attention to it and looked away from the road. This increased traffic accidents by 4.5% within 10 kilometers of the sign, amounting to an additional 2,600 crashes and 16 deaths per year.
5. Half the states in the USA have now deployed these signs.

Too much oil (2012)

1. There were too many people relying on oil for heating.
2. Ireland introduced subsidies for heating run on renewable sources.
3. People will do things that make them more money if it doesn't give any adverse consequences to them.
4. The rate paid by the subsidy was greater than the cost of the wood used, so farmers would heat empty buildings just to get the subsidy. This caused such a scandal that it made the Irish government collapse for almost 3 years.

Too many birds (2012)

1. Birds are congregating on the Texas Medical Center campus and leaving droppings everywhere.
2. The college covered their oak trees with nets for birds to go elsewhere.
3. Nature is an interconnected web with a food chain and many negative feedback loops.
4. The trees had a 7,300% increase of megalopyge opercularis, which is North America's most venomous caterpillar that causes awful symptoms if touched. This was especially bad given that the organization conducted medical research.

Bottle water waste (2013)

1. Bottled water consumption at the University of Vermont is creating too much waste.
2. The school tried to eliminate water bottle vending machines, hand out reusable containers, and spent $100,000 to add bottle filling stations around the campus.
3. People don't always remember to bring things with them.
3. Students often forgot their water bottles, sugary drink vending machine sales rose by 25%, and plastic bottle use increased by 6% per capita.

(2013)

1. There was a Wikipedia page about a classified French military radio station.
2. France's government demanded that Wikipedia delete the page.
3. A system that publishes information doesn't like to have it taken down.
4. Wikipedia didn't see a reason to do it because the information had been publicly available in an interview with a French Air Force major stationed there and refused to delete it without further clarification.
5. The French government summoned the president of Wikimedia France and threatened him with arrest and imprisonment. He deleted the entry, but also alerted others that reposting the page is a crime in France. A Swiss contributor restored it, and the controversy briefly made it the most viewed French Wikipedia page in the world.

Political ban (2016)

1. Other states in the USA were passing [conservative](politics-conservativeliberal.md) laws that the liberal city of San Francisco didn't like.
2. San Francisco prohibited any city contracts with companies headquartered in states that don't share San Francisco's values.
3. Prices are kept low through [competition in the market](money-economics.md).
4. Public projects rose by 20%. The ban also created about ~$550,000 in bureaucratic costs for hiring, as well as making it difficult to support like-minded organizations in banned states.
5. The list of rejected states grew from 4 to 30, and they had to issue many waivers and exceptions. The city killed the ban in 2023.

Cigarette ban (~2016)

1. Cigarettes are bad for your health.
2. Australia made cigarettes absurdly expensive.
3. Citizens will try to maintain their lifestyle if they can, which includes doing illegal things.
4. By 2025, 80% of all tobacco consumed in Australia was illegal, up from 12% in 2017.

Noise pollution (2018)

1. The noise was too loud in the city of Edmonton in Alberta, CA.
2. The Edmonton government placed sound meters all around the city indicating when their vehicle was making too much noise above the 85-decibel limit.
3. People like to compete for scores when there aren't adverse consequences to it.
4. Some people found it fun to see how high they could get their scores. Many more people called in to complain about the noise, and the government turned off the displays within weeks.

Gender pay equity (2019)

1. Across the board, men and women weren't making the same pay.
2. Colorado made many regulations that required all forms of compensation disclosed on job listings and bans on asking about employment history.
3. People will often choose the path of least resistance, which means looking for their needs elsewhere.
4. The new legal liabilities and administrative burdens made hiring more difficult, so most large companies simply excluded Colorado applicants for working remotely.

Tree shortage (2019)

1. There weren't enough trees in Mexico.
2. Mexico's government created a $3.5 billion project for farmers to plant trees on foreign land. It would also help poor workers.
3. People will do things that make them more money if it doesn't give any adverse consequences to them.
4. Poor farmers cut down existing forests and replanted them to get paid. It caused the deforestation of over 280 square miles.

Restaurant adoption (2019)

1. DoorDash wanted to become popular with restaurants, but restaurants weren't signing up for their program.
2. DoorDash decided to automatically add restaurants to their system without notification or permission.
3. People don't like being forced into things they don't [consent to](people-contracts.md).
4. Restaurateurs were upset about the violation of common decency. Restaurants that never offer delivery were getting complaints and bad reviews about orders arriving cold. AJ's Pizzeria in Kansas saw that his $26 specialty pizza was being sold for $16, so he ordered many of them himself, and often put raw pizza dough in the box to maximize his yield.
5. DoorDash lost $668 million in 2019.

Airline bailout (2020)

1. The airline industry was struggling due to the COVID-19 pandemic.
2. The goverment gave a $60 billion bailout to maintain continuity.
3. Companies have to stay profitable or they'll go out of business.
4. Airlines were mandated to maintain a minimum level of service to keep the government grants, so they were forced to fly planes even when there were no passengers on board ("ghost flights").

Vaccine distribution issues (2021)

1. The COVID-19 mRNA gene therapy vaccine doses were about to expire.
2. New York quickly created a barely functioning website to expedite everyone receiving the vaccine, then fine distribution centers for not distributing them to non-priority patients.
3. Sometimes administrative burdens are too difficult to manage, especially if it's against what people consent to do.
4. This became worse because they punitively fined all the medical providers and threatened to strip medical licenses and halt future vaccine deliveries for anyone who didn't fully comply. This was also in the midst of quite a few nursing home scandals and issues with the vaccines themselves.

Non-organic farming (2021)

1. Sri Lanka's farms weren't organic (i.e., not using pesticides).
2. Sri Lanka made a nationwide ban on synthetic fertilizers and pesticides. This also saved them $400 million in subsidy costs.
3. Synthetic pesticides and fertilizers do work well at [increasing yield](plants.md).
4. 6 months after the decree, Sri Lanka's exports plummeted. Tea was down 18% and rice was down 20%, which required $450 million to import rice. The public backlash was so severe that Sri Lanka had to pay hundreds of millions to compensate farmers. This was also also connected to the president resigning and being temporarily exiled.

Hero pay (2021)

1. Politicians wanted to gain more votes for veterans.
2. They implemented a mandatory "hero pay" raise of 28% in grocery stores.
3. Companies have to stay profitable or they'll go out of business.
4. Since most grocery stores run on a razor-thin margin, most lower-performing grocery stores went out of business and made those veterans unemployed.

High rent (2021)

1. The rent is too high in St. Paul, Minnesota.
2. St. Paul's government required that rent increases could only move upward by 3% every year, even if there was a change in occupancy.
3. Companies have to stay profitable or they won't go into business.
4. This law didn't acount for inflation, and applied to new apartment buildings. Multi-family building permits went down in St. Paul by 80% and almost doubled in the rest of the state. This also contributed to a growth in homeless encampments.

Sesame allergy (2022)

1. Many people were allergic to sesame seeds.
2. The FDA added a stricter requirement for product labels to indicate when food had or had possibly come into contact with sesame seeds, not just "may contain them".
3. Sesame seeds are extremely tiny.
4. To avoid legal liability, companies intentionally added sesame seeds to their products, thereby hurting the product selection for people with sesame seed allergies.

Gun buyback (2022)

1. Utica, NY has too many guns.
2. The government created an anonymous buyback program with a premium for "ghost guns" that didn't have serial numbers.
3. People will do things that make them more money if it doesn't give any adverse consequences to them.
4. A man with a [3D printer](engineering-printers.md) from out-of-state made over 100 guns and parts, which were all eligible for the program. He drove a few states over and gained $21,000 under the program.

Low wages (2022)

1. The base pay for workers receiving tips in Washington, DC was a fraction of the minimum wage. Employers were already required to pay the difference of tips and minimum wage to give them minimum wage already, but the government didn't see it as sufficient.
2. Initiative 82 introduced yearly pay increases for tipped workers until it reached minimum wage in 2027.
3. Companies have to stay profitable or they'll go out of business.
4. Many restaurants were forced to raise prices, cut hours, fire staff, or go out of business. Many establishments started directly charging Initiative 82 fees to the customer. The average tipped worker's wage dropped by an average of $1,800 per year.
5. In response, the government made the base pay raise every two years and the pay increases would go up to 75% of minimum wage by 2034.

Dirty jet fuel (2023)

1. Jet fuel creates pollution.
2. The Malaysian government gave incentives for aircraft to use used cooking oil instead of jet fuel.
3. People will do things that make them more money if it doesn't give any adverse consequences to them.
4. Since fresh palm oil was subsidized by the government, people realized they could make more money on used oil than fresh oil. Restaurant deep fryers were changed much more frequently, entrepreneurs were mixing fresh oil into used oil and calling it used, and at some point fresh oil was simply renamed used. This created an incentive that actually increased deforestation for palm oil farming.

Search dominance (2024)

1. Google's search dominance was being challenged by ChatGPT.
2. Google developed a [machine learning model](computers-ai-ml.md) that would give summaries of Google search queries off the internet.
3. Machine learning isn't intelligent, and only uses the training data it receives.
4. Google's model made false statements off the internet as if they were fact such as Barack Obama being Muslim, how to cook with gasoline, eating rocks, and adding glue to pizza.
