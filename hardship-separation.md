
# How to handle separation from loved ones

There are many reasons why a separation from a loved family member can happen.

- [Wars](people-conflicts-war.md)
- [Narcissistic abuse](hardship-abuse-narcissist.md)
- [Cult membership](people-culture-cults.md)

One of the most common forms of separation comes from a misapplication of the "no contact rule".

- In effect, separation in a [romantic relationship](relationships-dating.md) may be very difficult when the victim is being abused but still has feelings for their partner.
- However, many people use information they encounter on social media to justify outright estrangement.
- The only way to determine if it was a healthy severance or not was if the estranging person did everything possible to [reconcile and find a peaceable solution](people-conflicts-attitudes.md) before going no contact.
  - More often, separation was *not* ideal, and the estranging individual doesn't have the [emotional capacity](mind-feelings.md) to confront the conflict.
  - You and they will never find peace until the conflict is properly resolved.

## Don't blame yourself

Unless you can pinpoint what you did wrong, don't take their separation personally.

- Many times, people will choose to estrange themselves out of [fear](mind-feelings-fear.md) over what may happen otherwise.
- Other times, people can be [manipulated](people-image-distortion.md) into believing they're making the right decision.

Get your situation sorted out with [God](religion-answers.md).

- *You* don't have connection with them, but God certainly does.

To the best of your ability, focus on [personal healing](mind-feelings-sadness.md).

- It's very common to focus on them and aspects of their life ("rumination"), but it's not healthy.
- Frequently, you will need to take healing in the empty middle-time because of *increased* conflicts whenever you re-encounter them.

## When you are a victim of parental alienation

Do whatever you can to stay in touch.

- Even if it's mailing a letter, try to send information to them about how much you love them.
- They may not always receive it, but you will never know your impact until much later.

Parental alienation can be defined by 5 specific factors:

1. The child shows resistance or refusal to see the other parent.
2. There was a prior positive relationship between the child and rejected parent.
3. The neglected parent didn't show any abuse, neglect, or severely deficient parenting.
4. The preferred parent has shown several of 17 common alienating behaviors:
   1. Speaking badly about the rejected parent.
   2. Limiting the child's contact with the rejected parent.
   3. Interfering with the child's communications with the rejected parent.
   4. Limiting mention of the rejecting parent.
   5. Withholding approval when the child is interested in the rejecting parent.
   6. Telling the child the rejected parent doesn't love them.
   7. Allowing the child to choose between their parents.
   8. Creating the impression that the rejected parent is dangerous.
   9. Forcing the child to reject the alienated parent.
   10. Confiding with the child about adult topics.
   11. Asking the child to spy on the rejected parent.
   12. Asking the child to keep secrets from the rejected parent.
   13. Referring to the rejected parent by their first name.
   14. Referring to a step-parent as "mom" or "dad".
   15. Withholding medical, social, or academic information from the rejected parent.
   16. Changing the child's name to remove the association with the rejected parent.
   17. Undermining the authority of the rejected parent.
5. The alienated child has some or all of the 8 signs of parental alienation:
   1. A denigration campaign, where the child lists criticisms about the rejected parent to counselors, evaluators, attorneys and, ultimately, the judge.
   2. Weak, frivolous, and absurd rationalizations for the child rejecting the parent.
   3. No ambivalence whatsoever about the favored or rejected parent (i.e., one parent is all good and the other is all bad).
   4. The independent thinker phenomenon, where the child states their decision to cut off the other parent is theirs alone.
   5. No guilt about being rude or hurtful of their rejected parent.
   6. Reactive support for their parent in any conflict.
   7. Borrowed scenarios (i.e., making accusations about the rejected parent with phrases and ideas adopted from the favored parent).
   8. Rejecting the rejected parent's extended family.

Since you're facing a type of injustice from your [significant other](relationships-marriage.md), you're facing multiple issues:

1. The injustice from their actions against you.
2. The injustice from their actions against your child.
3. Social effects over when others express very little sympathy.
4. System-wide issues that come from courts and other authority structures.

To avoid being overwhelmed, try to take each allegation separately.

- You're guaranteed to lose [friends](people-friends.md) alongside the alienation, and that's [likely a good thing](people-friends-bad.md).
- The unjust actions against your children will come later, so focus on [recovering](hardship.md) from your [betrayal](hardship-betrayal.md) first and foremost.

In parental alienation your healing is the most important issue, since you may be facing a *lot* of enablers.

- It takes a unique type of [emotional maturity](mind-awareness-feelings.md) to accept that someone will make a false judgment of the facts.
  - Learn to accept that they have the right to their views, even when those views are working against truth.
- The story is frequently the same pattern of victimhood: the alienated parent has been estranged because it's the [morally](morality.md) best interests of the child.
  - Many people fall prey to this story because it's far more convenient than addressing the deeper conflict present in the situation.
- Many people will support one side of a family conflict without questioning the full story:
  - Well-meaning friends who listen and give advice
  - Profesionals like counselors, teachers, and social workers who accept one version of events without more thoroughly investigating
  - Extended family members
- While it seems unfair, the only way to avoid further estrangement is to learn [self-composure](mind-awareness.md) in the presence of many unfair allegations.
  - The alienating parent will do everything they can to portray a distressed parent as unstable and overly protective.

Complicit professionals won't always be obvious until more time has passed.

- Many third-party professionals will show clear signs of implicit enablement:
  - They'll immediately frame the case as a "50/50 conflict".
  - They'll prioritize cooperation at all costs.
  - They'll dismiss the emotional and psychological distress from the alienated parent.
  - Their focus will emphasize tone more than patterns of behavior.
  - They'll ignore child neglect and endangerment.
  - They'll dismiss any history of violence, protection orders, stalking, substance abuse, or sexual addictions.
- While it is difficult, make sure to document any discrepancies.
  - The professional should value patterns over isolated incidents.
  - They should review documentation carefully.
  - The professional should understand the impact of cumulative harm over time.

Sometimes it can happen through a dramatic event (such as a false allegation of a [crime](legal-crimes.md)), but it more often arises through a slow decay.

- Frequently, parental alienation is from a parent's constant, consistent, repeated allegations.
  - A pre-adolescent child depends on their parent to interpret the world, so they will typically take the information at face value as truth.
  - This will often rebuild a child's emotional landscape to make their outward behaviors feel like their natural choice instead of an imposed one.
- Frequently, the alienating parent will use *massive* information control.
  - Preventing the child from accessing their parent alone
  - Withholding important medical information from the alienated parent such as medical information or family outings
  - Excessively interrupting any time the parent has with the child
- The parent will use repeated cues to portray a negative story of the other parent.
  - A condescending tone of voice
  - Selective storytelling
  - Withholding information
  - "Concerned" comments for the children's safety
  - This can include absolute statements that are simply untrue:
    - "They didn't call you yesterday."
    - "They don't care about what you want."
    - "You're better off without them."
- You will often see a child echoing the parent's desires.
  - They'll often become argumentative and combative when they hadn't been before.
  - They may be entitled to receive parental sacrifices while also feeling arrogance over how they feel better than the parent.
  - They may be unable to recognize prior bonding experiences with the alienated parent.
  - At later stages, the child may simply refuse to interact with their parent and allege it was their idea all along.

Many times, courts can inadvertently magnify a parental alienation situation by not acting expediently.

- The entire focus of a "silver bullet" [divorce](hardship-family-divorce.md) is to override due process by manufacturing fear.
  - In practice, the "better safe than sorry" mindset can justify separating the child from a fit parent.
- As much as possible, occupy your time and energy with things you *can* control.
- The system *will* move slower than you'd prefer, so expect and learn patience.
  - Expect to have more evaluations, therapy, time away, and restrictions once the legal process begins.

Part of the lack of sympathy comes through how parental alienation abusers tend to state similar-sounding allegations from both the victims and the abusers.

- If a person were to hear both sides of a story, they'd see that both people are alleging mostly the same thing (e.g., they abused them, psychological abuse, etc.).
- Further, the victim, due to [how the final stage of narcissism affects people](hardship-abuse-narcissist.md), tends to be more silent, which means ~60-70% of parental alienation on social media is by the alienators.

[Court systems](hardship-family-divorce.md) are outside parties that don't know much, so don't expect them to be much help.

- From their point of view, one party is alleging abuse while the other party denies it, and the alleged victim is demanding things like child support and alimony.
- While it can be labeled a "high conflict case", it often means the courts will move more slowly just to be entirely sure of what they're doing.
- Ideally, child support should happen once contact has been resolved, but most courts don't see it that way.
- Keep asking to see your child.
  - It still hurts over time as you have to keep asking, but those requests can be easily flip to being proof in a court that you don't care to see the child.

Stay mindful that your extended family is hurt as well.

- It won't be as severe as your loss, but other family members have lost access to the child as well:
  - Grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins have been utterly erased from that child's life.

### Be prepared for the children to reject you

A child may suddenly reject their parent or behave coldly to them if something triggered it.

- For example, if the parent was violent, unreliable, or emotionally unavailable, the child may express what they have felt for a long time now that they feel safe to express it.

However, there are some very specific patterns that consistently arise when children have been manipulated by their other parent to hate the target parent:

- Sudden and rigid rejection of that parent (including not wanting to talk), but without a clear reason.
  - This can also include very little guilt or remorse for rejecting that parent.
- Speaking with borrowed language or phrases that seem scripted or out-of-place for that child's normal speech patterns.
- The child is unwaveringly loyal to their alienating parent, even when it hurts them.

Further, the alienating parent will have very clear indicators in response to the child.

- They will speak negatively about that parent in front of their child to undermine their character.
- They may restrict or control contact with the target parent without clear safety reasons.
- The parent may also at the same time be emotionally distant from their child, including neglecting their needs.
  - It's worth noting that they will sometimes *over*-perform in the presence of government authorities.
  - Motivationally, it's a [narcissistic](hardship-abuse-narcissist.md) desire to appear as favorable as possible in public while neglecting core needs.
  - They'll also often change or rewrite stories about the past to portray the other parent in the worst possible way.
  - Amidst their performance, they will also appear to be overly involved in the child's negative feelings toward the other parent.
- On a more advanced level, they'll create loyalty conflicts such as saying "if you loved me, you wouldn't go".

When they've been conditioned to say things, their phrases and behaviors have similar patterns.

- If they once had a warm, open relationship with a parent they'll often rapidly become distant, cold or even hostile to that parent.
- They use adult language instead of childlike expressions.
- They speak with black-and-white thinking, with no room for nuance.
- Their messages are repeated consistently, with little variance in their approach or tone.
- They'll appear to feel guilty around the estranged parent's affection.
- They'll make distance with extended family like grandparents or siblings.

The ruthlessness of the alienating parent will eventually show through, but it takes time.

- Many people will have a harder time spotting these discrepancies because they're outside the context.
  - Even professionals have a tendency to believe the best in others without knowing, so they will frequently assume a parent's concern is strictly protective.
- Most of them will try to manipulate the context to justify their actions, but children will eventually learn to see right through that manipulation (due simply to experience).

If you're a child who has had a divorcing parent, reach out to the other parent.

- You'll likely find out the truth for yourself, and it will be very different from the version of the story you heard from your custodial parent.

### Stay mindful of the children's hardship

A child's alienation will be harder on them than on the alienated parent.

- While a father or mother losing a child is awful, the child receives a more severe trauma from the estrangement.
- Further, a child who has been manipulated by their parent has had their boundaries violated about what they have the right to believe and think.
- In the presence of the alienating parent, a child isn't even safe to be [self-aware](mind-awareness.md) of their cognitive dissonance.

There is also a very dangerous risk when a child is in the custody of an alienating parent.

- On top of [lying](people-lying.md), an alienating parent will have already performed [a crime](legal-crimes.md) to get where they are.
  - Naturally, this becomes an easier [habit](habits.md) to reproduce later.
- In the case of specific [neurodivergence](mind-neurodivergence.md), if the parent is paranoid delusional (which is rare), they may harm or kill their child because they see it as a necessary measure to protect themselves and the child.

Most of what a child does in a separation is a pure survival instinct.

- They are forced into a situation where they must quietly accept their parent's version of the story to avoid adverse repercussions.
- When they're younger, this can often mean their entire [value system](values.md) will have to adapt to survive.

Childrens' memories are more malleable and easily influenced by emotions, suggestions, and repeated messages.

- Therefore, constant negative statements about a parent can easily register as true memories, even if they are inaccurate or exaggerated.
- Very often, children will carry this confusion far into adulthood, which represents [trust](mind-trust.md) issues with the alienated parent, trust issues with people, [relationship](relationships-dating.md) issues, and emotional pain due to distant or lost memories.

Even if a child *appears* to be decisive in the presence of the alienated parent, they have tremendous conflicts in their mind.

- They know the other parent isn't present, but haven't heard a good reason why and continually wonder.
- They aren't aware if they're even allowed to miss that parent.
- Frequently, they won't feel it's safe to even talk about the other parent during holidays or special events.

Often, children will find cracks in their estranging parent's narrative once they reach adolescence.

- They will frequently reassess the thoughts they were given, and will often develop a different view than they had before.
- Many times, they'll secretly reach out to the other parent or seek answers.

Their hardship won't stop at adulthood, however, and they will have to face some very ugly realities:

- They'll have [identity](people-identity.md) issues throughout their life from not having a healthy connection with both parents.
- They'll have [attachment](people-friends.md) issues because they can't [trust](mind-trust.md) strangers when their own parent was that [evi](morality-evil.md).
- They'll face tremendous [guilt](mind-feelings-shame.md) and [grief](mind-feelings-sadness.md) over their lost childhood and parental relationship.
- They'll also have other challenges that can include [anxiety](mind-feelings-fear.md), [depression](hardship-depression.md), and limited social bonds.
- On a farther level, they may often experience [severe character disorders](mind-neurodivergence-clusterb.md).

The best way to resolve this is through loving support as the parent, as well as long-term therapeutic intervention.

### Your conversations with them

Their statements are signals of [abuse](hardship-abuse-narcissist.md), and aren't conclusions they came to themselves.

- In some ways, recovering from parental alienation is [cult deprogramming](people-culture-cults.md).

Ask good questions to break the ice, especially after a long estrangement:

- Avoid vague questions:
  - How was your day? (big vague question that's generally bad for [small talk](cs-langs-specific-smalltalk.md) in general)
  - How was your night? (could be used against you in a high-conflict situation)
- Avoid specific questions:
  - Anything asking about names, locations, details, or other information you could use
- Try to ask fun and casual questions that are better for children:
  - What was the most memorable thing you smelled today? (very effective with boys)
  - What was the best thing that happened to you at school?
  - What was the most fun thing that happened to you?
  - What was the furthest that you kicked the ball?
  - Ask random trivia questions about everyday things, then give fun [science](science.md) facts about them.
- Make a game out of asking those questions while doing something else.
  - Bear in mind that many video chats will mirror the image, so make an easy demarcation of left and right if it's necessary.
  - Play tic tac toe while you're spending time remotely and talking with them.
  - Make basic math problems on a sheet of paper, then have them solve it.
  - Draw little things, then see if they can guess it.
  - If you can, you can often play video games with them (such as browser-based games).
  - Play Connect Four with the numbers indicated as 1-7 for the columns.
- After you've established rapport, ask the same series of 6 questions in the same order:
  1. What is something that made you happy today?
     - This begins with an association with a positive feeling.
  2. What is something that gave you a bad feeling today?
     - This makes it clear that [feelings](mind-feelings.md) are never "bad" even when actions can be.
  3. What is something that made you feel loved today?
     - If they can't answer it, you can often talk about how people show love even when they don't say it.
  4. What is something you learned today?
  5. What is something that made you feel important today?
  6. What is something you did to make someone else feel loved or important?
- If the child says they can't talk about it because the other parent would get mad, respect their decision.
  - You can often de-escalate by saying "I understand if that makes you feel unsafe, so moving on..."

The next time you see your children, expect them to state things they were conditioned to say.

- "I don't want to see you anymore" / "Stop contacting me"
  - This sounds like they're [setting boundaries](people-boundaries.md), but they're often reflecting intense pressure from other caregivers.
- "You've never been there for me" / "You're the reason why everything went wrong"
  - It appears to be a grievance or blame, but they're often repeating the phrase they heard from the alienating parent.
- "Mom/dad says you're dangerous"
  - While it may seem like they're concerned, they're simply relaying the internalized warnings they received.
- "I just feel uncomfortable around you"
  - They often do feel discomfort, but it comes from having to reconcile the new information you present compared to what they've been told.
- "I don't remember anything good"
  - The other parent's negative memories have often suppressed memories they once had.
- "I'm choosing this myself"
  - They've often been coached to state autonomous-sounding statements, but they're often acting under the influence of the alienating parent.
- "They would never lie to me"
  - This shows their dependency on one side of the story, but it often isn't simply out of [trust](mind-trust.md).
- "If you loved me, you'd leave me alone"
  - It appears to be a boundary, but is usually emotional conditioning tied to their rejection.

Your response to their behavior will heavily de-escalate their issues.

- Stay calm, predictable, and emotionally available so they know you're safe to approach.
- Use neutral, pressure-free communication that invites dialogue without judgment.
- While you don't have to agree to false stories or blame, validate their feelings (e.g., "I can see how that would hurt you a lot to believe that").
- Consistently keep showing up to built [trust](mind-trust.md) over time.
- If you need to track behaviors for therapy or legal advice, document the patterns privately.

### Your continued presence

Do what you can to maintain your relationship during the estrangement.

- Send them letters and gifts whenever possible.

Explicitly and clearly indicate your love for them and how that will never change.

- Make it clear that you would never choose to be away from them, and that it was not your choice.
  - Avoid speaking badly about the other parent, even when it's true.
- Let them know you're doing everything you can to reunite with them.
- Express how it hurts you that you're still apart because it hurts both of you.
- Make sure they know they are wanted, they are your priority, and that you're counting down the days until you see them again.
